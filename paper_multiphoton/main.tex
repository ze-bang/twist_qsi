\begin{abstract}
Quantum spin ice is expected to host an emergent photon, and every
analytic prediction of what neutrons should see treats that photon as
free: one sharp line carrying all of the spectral weight.  We compute
the spectrum exactly instead, diagonalizing the ring-exchange model in
the full spin-ice configuration space of 48- and 64-site pyrochlore
clusters.  The free-photon picture holds at long wavelength and fails
at the zone boundary, where a \emph{roton} takes over: at the $L$
point a mode falls to half the free-photon energy, carries half of the
neutron intensity, and is the lowest excitation of the model.  Its
origin is the one Feynman identified in liquid helium, with a single
ingredient replaced.  In helium a sound wave becomes a roton because
the liquid can no longer be compressed and must rearrange instead.
Here the emergent electric field is the spin itself,
$E=S^z=\pm\frac12$ on every bond, so the field is saturated: a wave
cannot be made by increasing $E$, only by rearranging which bonds
point which way.  That rearrangement twists the magnetic flux and
destroys ring-exchange coherence, and the excitation energy is exactly
the coherence destroyed.  Plaquettes lying in the wavefronts enclose no
flux from the wave and cost nothing, and at $L$ alone --- the one
zone-boundary momentum along a hexagon normal --- a quarter of all
plaquettes are of this type.  Two further consequences of compactness accompany the
roton: the two photon polarizations, exactly degenerate in the free
theory, are split by their mutual interaction, and $13$--$38\%$ of the
neutron weight moves into multiparticle states.  A selection rule ---
reversing all spins leaves the Hamiltonian invariant, and neutrons see
only the odd states --- sends the complementary even spectrum to Raman
scattering and ultrasound.  The zone-boundary feature experiments
should expect is therefore not the photon but the roton.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\emph{Introduction.}---In quantum spin ice the low-energy physics is
not made of spins but of a field.  Virtual exchange on the
two-in--two-out ice manifold of the pyrochlore
lattice~\cite{Castelnovo2008} flips the six spins around a hexagon,
and the resulting theory is a compact $U(1)$ lattice gauge theory with
an emergent photon~\cite{Hermele2004,Gingras2014,SavaryBalents2017,
RauGingras2019,Broholm2020}, confirmed by quantum Monte
Carlo~\cite{Shannon2012,KatoOnoda2015} and realized equivalently by
three-dimensional quantum dimer models~\cite{Sikora2009}.  The
dictionary is simple and exact: the local moment \emph{is} the
emergent electric field, $S^z=E$, so the neutron structure factor
$\Szz(\bq,\omega)$ measures electric-field fluctuations.  A gapless
linearly dispersing mode carrying little low-energy weight is the
signature now pursued in a growing family of
materials~\cite{Benton2012,Ross2011,Sibille2018,Gaudet2019,Gao2019,
Sibille2020,Smith2022,Poree2025,Gao2025,Gao2026}.

Every analytic prediction of that signature describes a \emph{free}
photon, and the approximation behind it is stated openly in the
literature.  The emergent magnetic field is an angle, so its energy is
a cosine; one assumes that averaging over fast fluctuations both
softens the discreteness of the spin variable and keeps the flux
small, expands the cosine to quadratic order, and arrives, in the
words of Benton \emph{et al.}, at ``the Hamiltonian for non-compact
quantum electromagnetism''~\cite{Benton2012,Hermele2004}.  Later
treatments of the neutron response inherit
it~\cite{Desrochers2023,DesrochersKim2023PRL}.  The approximation is a
statement about long wavelengths, and there it is justified: in three
spatial dimensions the deconfined phase does flow to free
electrodynamics, monopoles are gapped, and the corrections are
irrelevant.

At the zone boundary none of that protection applies, and the reasons
to worry are concrete: the emergent fine-structure constant is
large~\cite{Pace2021}, semiclassical treatments find order-one
anharmonic renormalizations~\cite{Kwasigroch2017,Kwasigroch2020,
Szabo2019}, and quantum Monte Carlo finds a zone-boundary linewidth
comparable to the whole photon bandwidth, explicitly ``not captured''
by the Gaussian theory~\cite{Huang2018}.  Monte Carlo cannot say what
replaces the photon, because recovering a spectrum from imaginary time
smears exactly the structure in question.  That is the gap we close.
The zone boundary is not a corner case: it is where the
neutron-accessible momenta lie.

\emph{The model, and why nothing is missing from it.}---We study the
ring-exchange Hamiltonian on the ice manifold,
\begin{equation}
 H=-K\sum_{p}\left(W_{p}+W_{p}^{\dagger}\right),\qquad
 W_{p}=S_1^+S_2^-S_3^+S_4^-S_5^+S_6^-,
 \label{eq:ring}
\end{equation}
summed over contractible hexagons.  $W_p$ reverses the six spins
around a hexagon when they alternate and annihilates the state
otherwise, so $H$ rewards \emph{resonance}: a configuration gains
energy by being connected, through flippable hexagons, to its
neighbors.  In gauge language $W_p=e^{iB_p}$ and Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring})
is $-2K\sum_p\cos B_p$, the full cosine, never expanded.  Every
difference from the free-photon theory below comes from that one
refusal.

Compact electrodynamics is normally written
$\frac{U}{2}\sum_\ell E_\ell^2-K\sum_p\cos B_p$, and
Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring}) appears to be missing the electric term.  It is
not.  For spin one-half the electric field is the spin,
$E_\ell=S^z_\ell$, so $E_\ell^2=\frac14$ on \emph{every} bond of the
ice manifold: there the electric term is a constant and generates no
dynamics.  Equation~(\ref{eq:ring}) is the whole gauge Hamiltonian at
this order.  This is worth saying plainly, because it is the physical
reason the problem is hard: spin-one-half quantum spin ice is compact
$U(1)$ gauge theory with \emph{zero} bare electric stiffness, the
extreme of strong coupling, and whatever stiffness the photon has is
generated by the ring dynamics
itself~\cite{Hermele2004,Benton2012,Kwasigroch2017}.  There is no
small parameter to tune.  (For spin $S>\frac12$, or in rotor and
quantum-link regularizations, $E^2$ is dynamical and the coupling can
be made weak; the spin-one-half magnet cannot be.)

Equation~(\ref{eq:ring}) conserves the winding of the spin
configuration around the periodic box, contains no spinons --- the
Hilbert space is the ice manifold --- and excludes the noncontractible
six-cycles responsible for torus artifacts in finite-cluster
calculations~\cite{ZhouKimMask}.  It does retain the full magnetic
sector, so weight beyond the photon could in principle be magnetic
rather than multi-photon; we test that below.

\emph{One operator does everything.}---Because $S^z=E$, the neutron
operator is also the photon creation operator, and this single fact
organizes what follows.  In the free theory $E(\bq)\vert0\rangle$
\emph{is} one photon, exactly, which is why that theory places all of
the weight on one line.  In the compact theory the same state is a
superposition of everything the probe can reach, and our question is
what that superposition contains.

The two fields have a plain meaning in spins, and we use both languages
throughout.  The electric field is the moment itself, $E_\ell=S^z_\ell$,
so an electric-field wave is a modulation of the up--down pattern.  The
magnetic flux $B_p$ threads a hexagon, and $\cos B_p$ is the
ring-exchange resonance: a hexagon with a large $\langle\cos
B_p\rangle$ is one whose six spins are flipping back and forth
coherently.  The ground-state energy is the sum of those resonances.

\emph{What replaces the photon.}---Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows the
exact $\Szz$ beside the free-photon prediction evaluated on the same
lattice, so that every difference is an effect of compactness alone.
Two diagnostics classify the levels.  Projecting the probe onto the
two transverse polarization directions of the free theory says which
photon branch a level belongs to.  The second uses the one other
coupling this model is conventionally given.  Write
\begin{equation}
 H(V)=-K\sum_p\!\left(W_p+W_p^\dagger\right)+V\sum_p n_p^{\rm flip},
 \label{eq:rk}
\end{equation}
where $K$ is the ring-exchange amplitude, which sets every energy
quoted here, and $n_p^{\rm flip}$ is the projector onto hexagons whose
six spins alternate --- the flippable ones --- so that $\sum_p n_p^{\rm
flip}$ simply counts them.  The $V$ term is the Rokhsar--Kivelson
potential~\cite{RokhsarKivelson1988}, the standard second axis of the
quantum-ice phase diagram~\cite{Shannon2012,Gingras2014}, and at $V=K$
it reaches the exactly solvable point where the ground state is the
equal-amplitude superposition of ice configurations.  We use it only
as an infinitesimal probe at $V=0$, because the Hellmann--Feynman
theorem makes its slope an exact census:
\begin{equation}
 \frac{d\omega_n}{dV}
 =\langle n|\!\sum_p n_p^{\rm flip}|n\rangle
 -\langle 0|\!\sum_p n_p^{\rm flip}|0\rangle ,
 \label{eq:hf}
\end{equation}
the number of flippable hexagons the excitation adds or removes
relative to the ground state.  It is a measurement of what the state
is made of, not a fitted parameter.

At $L$ the two dominant levels behave completely differently.  One
sits at $1.73K$, near the free-photon line at $2.03K$, and loses
flippable hexagons under the deformation: an ordinary dressed photon.
The other falls to $1.02K$, half the free-photon energy, takes $51\%$
of the neutron intensity, \emph{gains} flippable hexagons, and is the
lowest excitation of the entire model --- we scanned all $9104$ levels
of the ground sector, labeled by spin-reversal parity, and nothing of
either parity lies beneath it.  It is not two photons or three; those
are excluded by parity and by kinematics, and the flux-correlation
test below excludes a magnetic pair.  It is a roton, and the rest of
this paper is about what that means microscopically.

\emph{What an electric-field wave costs.}---Electric and magnetic
variables are conjugate, so an electric-field wave necessarily disturbs
the flux, and the ring exchange is the entire energy of the problem.
The cost is therefore magnetic, and for Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring}) it can be
written down in closed form.  The first moment of the structure factor
is
\begin{equation}
 f_1(\bq)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_p\big\langle W_p+W_p^\dagger\big\rangle
          \sum_\mu\big|g^\mu_p(\bq)\big|^2 ,
 \label{eq:f1}
\end{equation}
where $g^\mu_p(\bq)$ is the lattice curl --- the flux the wave drives
through hexagon $p$.  Equation~(\ref{eq:f1}) is exactly the Maxwell
magnetic energy $\sum_p({\rm stiffness})\times|\nabla\!\times\!A|^2$ of
the configuration the neutron creates, with one difference from the
usual lattice gauge theory: the stiffness is not a parameter but a
measured property of the ground state, $\langle
W_p+W_p^\dagger\rangle=2\langle\cos B_p\rangle$, the strength with
which that hexagon is already resonating.  This is the stiffness that
free-photon treatments must introduce by
hand~\cite{Hermele2004,Benton2012}; here it is computed.
Equation~(\ref{eq:f1}) contains no free parameter, reproduces the exact
spectral first moment at every momentum, and obeys $\sum_p\langle
W_p+W_p^\dagger\rangle=-E_0/K$ identically.  It plays the role that the
free-particle recoil $\hbar^2q^2/2m$ plays in helium.

In spin language the same statement reads: twisting the flux through a
hexagon spoils the coherence of the six-spin resonance there, and the
energy lost is the resonance strength times the square of the twist.

The energy of the state the neutron creates is then
$f_1(\bq)/S(\bq)$~\cite{Feynman1954}, where the equal-time structure
factor $S(\bq)$ measures how much of that electric-field pattern the
ground state already contains: where the field is already fluctuating
that way, exciting it costs less.  \emph{The magnetic cost, divided by
what was already there} --- that is the whole argument, and it is the
argument Feynman used for the helium roton.

\emph{Why at $L$.}---Two things happen there at once, one geometric
and one dynamical [Figs.~\ref{fig:main} and \ref{fig:mech}].

The geometric one is exact, and it is ordinary electromagnetism on a
lattice.  A hexagon lying \emph{in} a wavefront --- in a plane of
constant phase, perpendicular to $\bq$ --- encloses no net flux from
the wave: the curl $g_p$ vanishes identically, because the six sites
sit at one phase and the alternating signs of the loop cancel.  Such
plaquettes are threaded by nothing, so by Eq.~(\ref{eq:f1}) they
contribute no magnetic energy, and their resonance is left intact.
Each pyrochlore hexagon is perpendicular to one of the four
$\langle111\rangle$ axes, and $L$ is the only zone-boundary momentum
lying along such an axis.  There, one family of hexagons in four lies
in the wavefronts and drops out of the cost exactly, reducing $f_1$ to
three quarters of its value at neighboring zone-boundary momenta.  A
wave can be built at $L$ while a quarter of the plaquettes never
notice it.

The dynamical one is that $S(\bq)$ is largest at $L$
(0.370 at $L$ against 0.19--0.29 elsewhere).  A smaller cost divided by a larger head
start makes $f_1/S$ minimal at $L$ among all twelve cluster momenta,
and $L$ is the only momentum where it falls below the free-photon
line.

Neither effect is inherited from the ice rule.  Sampling the classical
ice manifold directly, on a $2048$-site pyrochlore, gives a structure
factor flat across the zone to within a few percent, and the
equal-amplitude ice state on our own cluster favors $L$ by only about
ten percent.  The interacting ground state concentrates far more
weight there while \emph{suppressing} other momenta.  Unlike the
helium roton, this one is not a property of the medium it lives in;
the ring dynamics manufactures it.

\emph{What the roton is made of.}---The exact state answers this
directly, and the answer is the same in either language.  Relative to
the ground state it carries \emph{less} magnetic coherence: $\sum_p
\langle\cos B_p\rangle$ falls, and the amount it falls by, $1.02K$, is
exactly the excitation energy.  The flux has spread away from
$B_p\simeq0$.  In spins, the six-spin resonances have gone out of phase
with one another.  At the same time the state contains \emph{more}
hexagons in a flippable arrangement ($+0.34$).  The two facts are not
in conflict --- how many plaquettes \emph{can} resonate and how
coherently they \emph{do} are different questions --- and together they
say what the roton is: a rearrangement of the electric-field pattern
that leaves more plaquettes able to fluctuate while destroying the
phase agreement among them.  It pays in coherence, not in ice-rule
strain, which is why it costs half of what stiffening the flux into a
photon costs.

A second stage happens where the neutron cannot look.  The bare
electric-field wave costs $1.45K$; the true roton costs $1.02K$, so the
system recovers $29\%$ of the price.  Resolved plaquette by plaquette,
$71\%$ of that recovery comes from the one family lying in the
wavefronts.  The reason is that those plaquettes carry none of the
wave: their flux can be rearranged \emph{without} reducing the state's
overlap with what the neutron created, so they are free to be spent.
The wave is built on three families of plaquettes and paid for largely
by reorganizing the fourth.  This is the lattice counterpart of
backflow in helium~\cite{FeynmanCohen1956}, where the liquid recovers
energy through motion the density wave does not specify, and it is why
the exact energy lies well below $f_1/S$
[Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}(a)].

\emph{Is this not simply another photon?}---Genealogically, it is: the
polarization decomposition connects the level continuously to the
second transverse branch of the free theory, and no new particle
appears from nowhere.  The situation is exactly that of helium, where
the roton is not a separate species but the high-momentum portion of
the very branch that starts as sound --- one dispersion curve whose
\emph{character} changes along the way.  ``Renormalized photon,''
however, would mean the same character with shifted numbers, and that
reading fails every test that probes character rather than ancestry.
A photon's energy \emph{falls} under the flippability bias; the
roton's \emph{rises} --- a sign flip, not a shifted magnitude
[Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}(b)].  A photon pays its energy in flux stiffness
while preserving ring-exchange coherence; the roton carries \emph{more}
flippable plaquettes with \emph{less} coherence.  A photon's energy is
set by the generated stiffness; the roton's is set by the equal-time
structure factor through $f_1/S$, tracking $S(\bq)$ class by class on
the 64-site cluster and point by point along the deformation.  And no
photon detaches from its band: the roton sits below the entire photon
spectrum and is the lowest excitation of the model.  The name marks a
change of character, not of ancestry, and the change is measured in
sign flips, not percentages.

\emph{Relation to known soft modes.}---The closest antecedents are the
``resonons'' of quantum dimer models.  At the fine-tuned
Rokhsar--Kivelson point the ground state is an equal-amplitude sum, its
correlations are those of the \emph{classical} dimer ensemble, and the
same single-mode logic then predicts soft modes wherever those
classical correlations peak~\cite{RokhsarKivelson1988}: on the square
lattice, soft resonons at $(\pi,\pi)$ and $(\pi,0)$, confirmed
dynamically~\cite{Lauchli2008}.  In three dimensions the same analysis
finds nothing of the kind: at the cubic-lattice RK point the only soft
mode is the remnant of the photon itself~\cite{Lauchli2008}, because
the classical Coulomb-phase correlations are featureless --- the same
flatness we measure for the classical ice manifold.  The roton
reported here therefore has no RK-point ancestor.  It lives deep in
the deconfined phase with no fine-tuning, at finite energy rather
than at a critical zero, and it exists only because the quantum ring
dynamics manufactures the correlation peak that the classical
ensemble lacks.  Where the resonon is a critical precursor read off
from classical correlations, this roton is an interaction effect of
the deconfined gauge field itself.

\emph{Why compactness is what makes this happen.}---In the free theory
$E$ is a continuous field and a photon is a small-amplitude
oscillation of it: the electric field grows a little, the flux
stiffens a little, and the two exchange energy indefinitely.  In the
magnet $E=S^z=\pm\frac12$ on every bond, so the field is
\emph{saturated}.  A wave cannot be made by increasing $E$ anywhere,
because there is nowhere for it to go; it can be made only by
rearranging which bonds carry which sign, and that rearrangement is
exactly what changes the flux and costs the resonance.  At long
wavelength the rearrangement can be spread over many bonds and is
indistinguishable from a small oscillation --- which is why the free
photon survives in the infrared and why the multiparticle weight falls
toward small $\bq$.  At the zone boundary the two part company, and the
rearrangement is the cheaper option.  \emph{The roton is what an
electric-field wave becomes when the field can no longer change its
magnitude, only its arrangement.}  In helium the binding constraint is
that the liquid cannot be compressed below the interparticle spacing;
here it is that a spin one-half cannot exceed one-half.  The
constraint differs; the consequence is the same.

\emph{Evidence that this is one mode and not an accident.}---Three
checks.  First, the mode is collective: improving the electric-field wave
variationally --- the systematic version of adding backflow ---
converges onto the exact energy within a few steps at $L$, faster than
at any other momentum ($1.450\to1.074\to1.034\to1.026K$, against $1.024K$ exact).  Second, the mechanism
predicts \emph{which} momenta rotonize.  On the 64-site cluster the
$L$-type momenta split into two inequivalent classes, and the class
with the larger $S(\bq)$ develops the low mode carrying half the
intensity while the other stays photon-dominated: rotonization follows
the structure factor, not the label $L$.  Third, it predicts how the
mode moves.  Deforming toward more flippable states leaves $f_1$
constant to under a percent while $S(L)$ grows, and the roton softens
monotonically as $f_1/S$ requires [Fig.~\ref{fig:mech}(b)], from
$1.02K$ to $0.73K$ without condensing in the range scanned.  With
$f_1$ fixed, the softening is not merely correlated with the growth of
$S$; it is caused by it.

\emph{The other two consequences of compactness.}---Compactness does
two more things visible in Fig.~\ref{fig:main}.  The free theory makes
the two transverse polarizations exactly degenerate at every momentum
of this cluster; the exact spectrum splits them, by under a percent at
the smallest momentum and by order one at the zone boundary.  Because
the degeneracy is exact in the free theory, the splitting measures the
photon--photon interaction directly.  And beyond both branches,
$13$--$38\%$ of the neutron weight sits in higher states that the free
theory does not contain at all.  Their character is multiparticle
rather than magnetic: the plaquette flux correlations of every bright
state are weak and spatially spread out, without the localized
disturbance a bound monopole pair would produce, and their response to
the deformation matches sums of single-mode responses.  This weight
falls to $13\%$ at the smallest momentum reachable, as the recovery of
the free-photon description at long wavelength requires.

Leading-order perturbation theory in the quartic term of the cosine
accounts for none of this.  Evaluated on the same modes, it predicts
polarization splittings and transferred weight below $0.1\%$ at the
coupling this model generates, and a few percent even with the
coupling tripled --- one to two orders of magnitude short.  The
photon--photon problem here is not a perturbation of the free theory.

\emph{A rule that divides the labor between experiments.}---Reversing
every spin leaves Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring}) unchanged, so every eigenstate is
even or odd under it, and $S^z$, being odd, connects the ground state
only to odd states.  (In gauge language this is charge conjugation,
and an $n$-photon state carries parity $(-1)^n$.)  The rule is exact,
and we confirm it at machine precision: every even level carries
neutron weight below $10^{-28}$.  Its consequence is practical.  The
two-photon continuum, and the even half of the spectrum generally, is
\emph{invisible} to neutrons and appears instead in probes coupling to
pairs of spins --- Raman scattering~\cite{Fu2017} and
ultrasound~\cite{Simon2022}.  Figure~\ref{fig:channels} shows both
channels; no level appears in both.  A bilinear-channel onset at twice
the neutron line would identify the emergent photon pair with no
adjustable parameter.  Stray-field noise
magnetometry~\cite{Naik2026} couples to the magnetization and so sits
on the neutron side of the division.

\emph{Consequences for experiment.}---Free-photon fits to neutron data
are least reliable exactly where the accessible momenta lie.  At the
zone boundary between a sixth and two thirds of the weight is not in
the one-photon line; at $L$ the photon retains only a third of it; and
the line itself is split.  The dominant zone-boundary feature is the
roton, at half the energy the free theory assigns to the photon there,
so a measured low-energy peak should not be read as a photon whose
speed has been renormalized.  The zone-boundary damping reported by
quantum Monte Carlo~\cite{Huang2018} acquires a concrete assignment:
weight moved into the split doublet and the multiparticle states.
Because the theory is gapless, multi-quantum continua reach down to
the one-photon line in the thermodynamic limit, so lineshape rather
than peak counting is the appropriate analysis.

\emph{Limitations.}---Two cluster sizes.  The roton's energy, its
share of the weight, and the mechanism are stable between them; the
splitting at $X$ is not, and we do not build on it.  We locate the
roton at the momenta the clusters provide but do not predict its
position throughout the zone: that requires $S(\bq)$ of the
interacting ground state at momenta these sizes cannot reach, and the
classical ice manifold cannot be substituted for it.  Since
Eq.~(\ref{eq:f1}) holds at any $\bq$, that one ingredient would turn
the mechanism into a zone-wide prediction.  Longer ring exchanges
present in microscopic models lie outside Eq.~(\ref{eq:ring}).  The
connection to those models is not made here: a companion analysis of
the XXZ model with torus artifacts removed~\cite{ZhouKimMask} finds
comparable off-pole weight at $L$, but there the photon-band states
carry virtual-spinon admixture and the comparison is deferred; the
overall scale $K$ can be targeted
thermodynamically~\cite{ZhouAnKim2026}.

\emph{Choice of cluster.}---One technical point deserves recording,
because it affects any small-cluster study of this problem.  The
standard 16- and 32-site pyrochlore cells cannot be used.  On the
32-site cell every configuration of the connected zero-flux sector has
exactly eight flippable hexagons, which makes the equal-amplitude
state an exact eigenstate and the spectrum integer-valued; on the
16-site cell only six transport sectors have any dynamics at all.
Neither supports the interacting photon problem.  We therefore use the
$2{\times}2{\times}3$ cluster (48 sites, $87\,546$ ice states, ground
sector of dimension $9104$, diagonalized completely, so the weights
saturate the equal-time correlator identically) and, for the size
check, $2{\times}2{\times}4$ (64 sites, $2\,756\,394$ ice states,
ground sector $249\,180$, by Lanczos after reproducing every 48-site
benchmark).  Momentum assignments are certified by exact translation
projectors.

\emph{Conclusion.}---Treating the compactness of the emergent gauge
field exactly changes what quantum spin ice is predicted to show.  The
free photon survives at long wavelength, where the field can be tipped
gently.  At the zone boundary, where it cannot, the response is
dominated by a roton at half the free-photon energy, carrying half of
the neutron weight and forming the lowest excitation of the model.
The mechanism is Feynman's, adapted to a saturated field: a wave that
cannot be made by increasing $E$ must be made by rearranging it, and
the price is the ring-exchange coherence that rearrangement destroys.
Alongside the roton the photon line splits and a multiparticle
continuum appears, and an exact parity rule routes half the spectrum
to non-magnetic probes.  Seen from outside magnetism, these are exact
strong-coupling spectra of a compact $U(1)$ gauge theory of the kind
quantum simulators are being built to
approximate~\cite{Bauer2023,Xu2026glueball}, obtained here from a
gauge field that a magnet supplies for free.
